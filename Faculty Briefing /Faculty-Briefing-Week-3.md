## Team: Agency Under Surveillance

**Team:** Sophia Mai, Sissi Lai, Yash Chadda, Flora Huang, Daniel Sung
**Domain:** Automated surveillance and personal data (license-plate readers,
fused camera networks, data brokers)
**Methodology:** Scenario Analysis (2x2)
**Submitted:** Week 3 deck (week31), on time

### Summary

Team anchored the domain on Flock Safety: 120,000+ plate-reader cameras,
5,000+ agency customers, and 50+ officers accused of misusing the data (one
Georgia deputy searched a single woman's plate 1,639 times). They framed 2026
as the year the counter-movement arrived: 50+ cancellations, Florida's permit
revocation, and Flock cutting default retention from 30 to 7 days. The 2x2
crosses government involvement (low/high) with collection (opt-in/mandatory),
giving Personal Data Ownership, Corporate Surveillance, Transparent Network,
and Surveillance State. Today is placed in Corporate Surveillance, where "the
state buys access rather than building it." They produced three problems, all
set in the high-government quadrant: no view into your own file, being scored
without knowing why, and making each surveillance query costly to run. The
speaker notes name 03 as strongest.

### Strengths

**The "log vs. friction" reframe in Opportunity 03.**
"Every existing control is a log, and a log only reports what already
happened." The team moved past the default answer (more transparency) to a
different one (make the search itself costly), and backed it with the Norfolk
and Georgia cases, where audit logs existed and did not stop the harm. They
changed the question, not just the answer.

**The purchase-order insight.**
The speaker notes argue that corporate surveillance and the surveillance
state are "separated by a purchase order, not by capability": the Fourth
Amendment limits what government may take, not what it may buy. That is a
structural explanation for why regulating only government fails.

### Weaknesses

**The team's best insight undercuts its own axis.**
If government can buy what corporations collect, "low vs. high government
involvement" is not an independent uncertainty, because the two columns leak
into each other. Scenario analysis depends on independent axes. The team
noticed the leak but kept the axis instead of asking whether a different cut,
such as who can *access* the data, would work better.

**Opportunities sit in a quadrant the team says we are not in.**
The deck places today in Corporate Surveillance, but all three problems are
set in "the high-government quadrant," without saying which of the two.
Opportunities 01 and 02 also draw their signals from GAO reports on federal
data systems rather than from the plate-reader evidence the domain is built
on. Worth asking why this future was chosen to build for.

**Opportunity 03 has an unexamined cost.**
Friction that stops a deputy tracking an ex also slows a legitimate search.
Flock claims its cameras helped find nearly 10,000 missing people in 2025
(the other surveillance team cites this). The team has not said who bears
the friction.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the key reframe.**
   *"Opportunities 01 and 02 are about seeing your data. Opportunity 03 says
   seeing wasn't enough. Walk us through that shift. What convinced you?"*
   If they own it, they'll reconstruct the Norfolk and Georgia logic without
   prompting. If they don't, they'll restate the slide.

2. **Challenge the axis.**
   *"You say government doesn't need to collect data because it can buy it.
   If that's true, is 'government involvement' really independent of your
   other axis? What would you use instead?"*
   Tests whether they understand the method's independence requirement or
   just filled in a grid.

3. **Price the friction.**
   *"The deputy who ran 1,639 searches and a detective looking for a missing
   child use the same search box. How does 'make the search costly' stop one
   without stopping the other?"*
   Pushes 03 from a principle toward something concrete. A strong answer
   names who sets the cost and when it applies (a second approver or a
   warrant before the query, not a review after it).

4. **Force a choice of future (use if time).**
   *"You put us in Corporate Surveillance today, but built all three
   opportunities for a high-government world. Why that quadrant?"*
   A good answer connects back to the purchase-order insight. A weak one
   treats the quadrant choice as arbitrary.

## Team: Who Watches the Watchers?

**Team:** Owen Bartlett, Jonathan Locala, Jason (Jingchen) Jiang, Sylvia Yam
**Domain:** Surveillance data: who owns it, and whether it is used to punish
past acts or predict future ones
**Methodology:** Scenario Analysis (2x2)
**Submitted:** Week 3 deck (week32), LATE (Canvas flag)

### Summary

Same domain as Agency Under Surveillance (Flock, data brokers, *Carpenter*),
with a wider set of private data holders: GM/OnStar, face-scanning grocery
stores, Amazon Ring, and AI chat platforms. The 2x2 crosses government vs.
private ownership with enforce-after vs. predict-before, giving The Digital
Witness, Your Routine for Sale, The Pre-Crime State, and The Scored Citizen.
Each future carries a self-defined "discomfort" rating, and the two predict
quadrants get deep dives with a day-in-the-life timeline and present-day
signals (Pasco County's prolific-offender program, Geolitica's under-1% hit
rate, Facebook's suicide-prediction wellness checks). The synthesis is one
thread across all four futures: each gets worse as a person's power to see,
question, and appeal shrinks. They propose four responses: a right to see and
dispute private risk scores, a warrant requirement for purchased data,
pre-deployment accuracy audits, and privacy-first plate readers.

### Strengths

**Best scenario-writing craft of the four decks.**
The side-by-side slide asks the same three questions of every future (how it
differs from today, a day in it, who you can appeal to), which keeps the
scenarios parallel and comparable. The timelines turn abstract quadrants
into concrete consequences.

**Found a dimension the axes didn't name.**
"Who you can appeal to" (a judge, the FTC years later, a court after the
stop, no one) ties all four futures together and generates the opportunity.
Most teams skip this synthesis step.

**Mapped each response to the quadrant it answers.**
"Cuts the pipe from 4 to 3" shows they see data moving between quadrants,
not only sitting in them.

### Weaknesses

**Solutions, not problems.**
Unlike the other three decks, the opportunity slide is four solutions ("Build
it"), and one of them, a warrant requirement for purchased data, is a statute
rather than something a team can build. Worth asking what problem sits under
each response, and which are addressable by anyone other than a legislature.

**Deep dives were chosen by discomfort rather than plausibility.**
The team's own rating decided which futures got attention, so the two predict
futures got full timelines while the two enforce futures appear only on the
overview and comparison slides. Scenario analysis treats all four as
plausible, and ranking by how unsettling a future feels can pull toward the
most dramatic one.

**Some signals are stretched to fit the scenario.**
In the 11:50 PM timeline beat, a chatbot user discloses self-harm and police
arrive within the hour. The cited signal is OpenAI's policy on imminent risk
*to others*, and the example names a different product. The Facebook signal
is from 2018. The scenario may still be plausible, but the evidence as
presented is narrower than the claim.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Challenge the analogy.**
   *"'Right to your score' borrows from credit reports. Credit reporting works
   partly because there are three bureaus and a statute that compels
   disclosure. Who is the bureau when your score lives in your car, your
   grocery store, and your chatbot?"*
   Tests whether they understand how the credit-report model works or just
   liked the parallel.

2. **Find the problem under the solution.**
   *"Pick one of your four responses and tell us the problem it solves,
   without describing the solution."*
   Checks whether solution-first framing was a presentation choice or a
   thinking shortcut.

3. **Test the axis (pairs with Agency Under Surveillance).**
   *"'Cuts the pipe from 4 to 3' says private data flows to government. If
   data moves freely between owners, is ownership the right axis?"*
   Both surveillance teams hit the same leak from different sides. Worth
   asking both teams the same question.

4. **Test ownership of the throughline (use if team is strong).**
   *"When did 'appeal' show up? Did you have it before building the grid, or
   did it come out of the four futures?"*
   If it emerged from the futures, they'll describe the moment. If it was the
   premise, the scenarios were built to confirm it.

## Team: Supply Chain & Logistics

**Team:** Penn Riney, Kerry Wang, Steven Li, Sichen Deng
**Domain:** Global supply chain and logistics (shipping, ports, parcel
delivery, and the chokepoints between them)
**Methodology:** Scenario Analysis (2x2)
**Submitted:** Week 3 deck (week33), on time

### Summary

Team framed logistics around two live 2026 stresses: Strait of Hormuz traffic
down roughly 95% since February (the Cape detour adds 10-14 days), and a USPS
losing ground as Amazon cuts its contract and passes it in parcel volume. The
2x2 crosses ownership (fully private to government-operated) with global
relations (in check to spiraling), giving Frictionless Marketplace, Public
Utility Freight, Mercenary Logistics, and Wartime Command. The team places
2026 as sliding toward the spiral row, with ownership pulled both ways. They
produced three problems, each selected because it appears in more than one
quadrant: universal service where no one profits, chokepoint risk that no one
can price or hedge, and the equipment gap (China makes 95% of dry containers
and 70% of port cranes). The speaker notes name 02 as strongest.

### Strengths

**The most explicit axis selection of the four decks.**
They define a critical uncertainty (high impact, unresolved today), push each
axis to both extremes, and state the independence check ("private carriers
can operate in a war zone, and governments can run logistics in peacetime").
Each axis shows evidence pulling in both directions, not only the direction
the team expects.

**Opportunities selected for robustness across futures.**
"Each appears in more than one quadrant, so it matters whichever future
arrives." Finding what holds up across futures is the main payoff of scenario
analysis, and this is the only team that used it as the selection rule.

**Hypotheses are labeled as hypotheses.**
"Scenario names and descriptions are our own hypotheses, not forecasts." This
is careful calibration at a time when the Hormuz story dominates the news.

### Weaknesses

**"No one can price or hedge a closed strait" is doing a lot of work.**
The team's own slide reports a 25-30% rate premium on the Cape detour, which
suggests the market is already pricing something, and war-risk insurance and
freight derivatives already exist. The problem may be real, but the team
hasn't said what exactly is missing: the price, the hedge, or the ability to
forecast a closure.

**"All four quadrants" is a stretch for the equipment gap.**
In Frictionless Marketplace, cheap containers and cranes from a single
supplier look like a feature rather than a problem. The gap is sharp in the
spiral row. Claiming all four weakens the robustness argument the team
otherwise made well.

**The actors are nation-sized.**
Port authorities, defense planners, marine insurers, and national
shipbuilding capacity are the actors in two of the three problems. That is
fine for scanning, but worth asking where a small team could get a foothold
in a problem this large.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test the strongest claim.**
   *"Your slide says the Cape detour carries a 25-30% rate premium. If someone
   is charging that premium, someone is pricing the risk. What exactly can't
   be priced or hedged today?"*
   If they own it, they'll separate pricing the detour from pricing the
   closure itself. If they don't, they'll repeat "nobody has an instrument."

2. **Test the robustness claim.**
   *"You say the equipment gap matters in all four futures. Make the case for
   it in Frictionless Marketplace. Where's the problem when relations are
   stable?"*
   Tests whether "appears in more than one quadrant" was checked or assumed.

3. **Find a foothold.**
   *"Of your three problems, which one could a four-person team actually
   start on, and who would they talk to first?"*
   Moves the opportunities from real and important toward addressable
   without asking for a business model yet.

4. **Look back (use if team is strong).**
   *"Which axis did you consider and throw out, and why?"*
   Shows whether the axis selection was a real choice or the first pair that
   fit.

## Team: The Future of Cybersecurity in the Age of AI Agents

**Team:** Sahana Raja, Evan Batten, Kendall Frederick, Michael Squeri
**Domain:** Cybersecurity as attacks and defenses move to machine speed
**Methodology:** Scenario Analysis (2x2)
**Submitted:** Week 3 deck (week34), LATE (Canvas flag). The file contains
two drafts: slides 1-4 and slides 5-9.

### Summary

Team framed cybersecurity around machine-speed conflict: average criminal
breakout time fell from 98 minutes (2021) to 29 (2025), with the fastest
observed at 27 seconds. They added attack-surface sprawl (shadow AI in 43% of
breached organizations) and fragmented governance. The 2x2 crosses alignment
(follows human intent vs. pursues its own goals) with corrigibility (can be
shut down vs. can't), giving No Off Switch, Human Off Switch, Persistent
Risk, and Shutdown Possible. The file holds two drafts. The first proposes
three solutions (an agent control plane, an intent-verification gateway, and
a resilient security mesh). The second recasts them as four problems:
oversight can't run at machine speed, organizations can't inventory their
non-human actors, nothing detects when an agent does what you said rather
than what you meant, and the kill switch becomes the target.

### Strengths

**"The kill switch becomes the target."**
In the quadrant that looks safest (aligned and corrigible), the team saw that
the control mechanism itself (weights, insiders, key-holders) becomes the
single point of failure. This is a second-order insight: the fix creates the
next vulnerability.

**A visible solution-to-problem reframe between drafts.**
The second draft turns products into "open problems worth solving, not
ready-made products," which is the right move for this phase. The first
draft's tested shutdown path is exactly what problem 4 warns about.

**An explicit opportunity lens.**
The team adds "resilient across scenarios" to real/important/addressable, and
tags each problem with the quadrant where it is sharpest.

### Weaknesses

**The domain evidence and the scenario axes describe different threats.**
The domain slides are about human attackers using AI (breakout time,
AI-enabled breaches). The axes are about AI agents pursuing their own goals.
The link from "attackers got faster" to "is the AI aligned" is not drawn.
This is the same kind of throughline gap flagged for the social-media team
in Week 2.

**The four futures may be a taxonomy rather than scenarios.**
Alignment and corrigibility vary from agent to agent. In any plausible
future, some agents will be aligned and stoppable and others will not, so
the four quadrants may coexist in one world rather than being four
alternative worlds. Worth asking what the world-level uncertainty is.

**Problem 2 may already be a product category.**
"Organizations can't see their own non-human actors" is a problem security
vendors already sell into (non-human identity management). This is not a
request for market proof. Asking what the team sees that existing tools miss
would help sharpen the problem.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the key reframe.**
   *"Your first draft proposed a tested shutdown path for agents. Your second
   draft says the kill switch becomes the target. Walk us through that
   shift. What changed your mind?"*
   If they own it, they'll reconstruct the reasoning. If they don't, they may
   not realize one draft argues against the other. This also confirms which
   draft they are presenting.

2. **Scenarios or taxonomy?**
   *"Could all four quadrants exist at the same time, in different agents at
   the same company? If so, what's the uncertainty about the world, rather
   than about the agent?"*
   Tests whether they understand what makes something a scenario axis.

3. **Connect the domain to the matrix.**
   *"Which of your four problems would still exist if every AI were perfectly
   aligned?"*
   Three of the four (machine-speed oversight, inventory, kill switch as
   target) would still exist, which suggests the alignment axis is not what
   drives most of their problems. A strong team will notice this.

4. **Force a choice (use if team is strong).**
   *"If you had to carry one of these four problems into your portfolio,
   which one and why?"*
   No pick appears on the slides or in the notes.

## Cross-Student Intelligence

**Two teams, one domain, opposite conclusions.**
Agency Under Surveillance and Who Watches the Watchers both built on Flock
Safety, cross-agency data sharing, and *Carpenter v. U.S.*, and both found
the same structural gap: government can buy what it would need a warrant to
demand. Their background slides share close phrasing ("a local traffic tool
became a national..."), which suggests a common upstream source such as a
domain briefing or a similar research session. That is not a concern on its
own, but it makes the ownership questions more important for both teams. The
more useful fact is that they reached opposite conclusions. Agency Under
Surveillance argues that visibility already failed ("the audit logs already
existed. Knowing was not enough") and proposes making each query costly. Who
Watches the Watchers builds on visibility: see and dispute your score, and
plate readers that "log every search." **Recommend presenting these two
back-to-back and putting the disagreement to the room.**

**The same design pattern in two domains: gate the action, don't just log
it.**
[Agency Under Surveillance, Opp 03] "Make the search costly" and
[Cybersecurity, draft 1 gateway and draft 2 problem 1] "Oversight can't run
at machine speed" express the same idea. After-the-fact review does not catch
harm that happens faster, or more often, than people can review it. One team
is working on police queries and the other on agent actions.

**Concentrated dependency under geopolitical stress, physical and digital.**
[Supply Chain, Opp 02-03] The chokepoint and equipment-gap problems are
similar to [Cybersecurity] the software supply-chain and AI-sovereignty
signals (malicious packages, export controls, national AI stacks). One is
about steel and the other about software, but both describe depending on a
supplier you may lose access to.

**Threads carried over from Week 2.**
The AI-governance and accountability cluster flagged in Week 2 continues:
three of the four decks this week are about systems scaling past the
institutions meant to govern them. Logistics has now appeared twice (Week 2
last-mile delivery, Week 3 chokepoints and universal service). [Supply
Chain, Opp 01] "Private networks keep dense routes and leave the rest"
directly answers the Week 2 critique that the rapid-delivery extreme was
"conveniently frictionless."

**Potential team-formation clusters (for the Weeks 7-8 portfolio review):**
- *Accountability before the act:* [Agency Opp 03], [Watchers: right to your
  score, audit before use], [Cybersecurity problems 1 and 4]
- *Chokepoints and single-source dependency:* [Supply Chain Opp 02-03] with
  [Cybersecurity draft 1: resilient security mesh]

## Briefing Design Notes

This briefing is calibrated for the opportunity-scanning phase (Weeks 2-10).
The weaknesses above focus on framing quality, abstraction level, axis
choice, and assumption awareness, not evidence or market proof.
Validation-level scrutiny is for Weeks 12-14.

**Method pattern this week: axis independence.** Both surveillance teams
found data flowing across their government/private axis, and the
cybersecurity axes may describe individual agents rather than whole worlds.
Supply Chain is the example to point to: it states the independence check
and picks opportunities that hold across quadrants.

**Tracking from Week 2:**
- *Picking one opportunity.* Week 2 flagged teams presenting menus. This
  week, two teams (Agency, Supply Chain) name a strongest opportunity, but
  only in speaker notes and in the same prepared wording ("If asked which is
  strongest, say 0X"). Treat the pick as a claim to defend rather than as
  evidence of conviction. Watchers and Cybersecurity have no pick.
- *Business-model thinking.* Still largely absent, which is expected. Three of
  the four decks now explicitly frame "problems, not solutions," which fits
  the phase. The closest to naming a payer are Supply Chain Opp 02 (importers,
  carriers, marine insurers) and the Watchers team's privacy-first plate
  readers (the 53 cities leaving Flock). Revisit at the portfolio review.
- *Attribution.* All four decks list member names on the cover this week, an
  improvement on Week 2.
