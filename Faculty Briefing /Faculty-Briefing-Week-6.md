# Faculty Briefing -- Week 6

*Monday-morning briefing, one card per team, generated from the four Week 6
domain decks. All four teams applied Jobs to Be Done: separate
the job from today's solution, map how the job gets done step by step, rate
each step on importance and satisfaction, and look for opportunities where
importance is high and satisfaction is low. Calibrated for the
opportunity-scanning phase (Weeks 2-10). The weaknesses below are about
framing quality, abstraction level, how the job is separated from the
solution, and assumption awareness, not evidence or market proof. Validation
scrutiny is for Weeks 12-14.*

## Team: Education Technology

**Team:** Flora Huang, Steven Li, Sylvia Yam, Penn Riney, Yash Chadda (first
names on the cover slide)
**Domain:** Education technology, with assigning, grading, and feedback as the
chosen job
**Methodology:** Jobs to Be Done
**Submitted:** Week 6 deck (week61), on time

### Summary

Team opened with the size of the grading burden: a median of 5 hours of a
54-hour teaching week goes to grading and feedback, 62% of teachers call
grading one of the worst parts of the job and 95% take it home, and teachers
who use AI tools weekly report saving about 6 hours a week. Slide 2 states the
framing directly: "The job is stable but the tools are split across it," with
Canvas holding the assignment and gradebook, Gradescope the rubric and
regrades, and Piazza or Slack the questions. Of three jobs (communication,
assigning and grading, collaboration), they chose the second because it
"touches every course and every student," with the desired outcome that
students can easily see how they're doing. The job is mapped in eight steps
across instructor, student, and both, rated 1 to 5 by the team. Three
opportunities come from steps rated high importance and low satisfaction:
make expectations clear the first time (step 3), one home for questions tied
to the work (step 4), and fast, consistent grading with real feedback (step
7). No strongest pick is named.

### Strengths

**Separated the job from the tools, in one line.**
"The job is stable but the tools are split across it." Each of the three jobs
on slide 3 is written apart from today's tools, which are listed separately
underneath. The slide also explains why the job feels badly served even
though every step has a tool: the steps are split across three products.

**The map crosses roles.**
The eight steps move between instructor, student, and both, so the map shows
where the job passes from one person to the other. The lowest-rated steps sit
on both sides: understanding the assignment (student, 5/2), deciding how to
grade and grading (instructor, 4/2 and 5/2), and regrades (both, 3/2).

**Each opportunity traces back to a rated step.**
Every card names its step, gives today's pain in one line, and then the
opportunity. The notes tie the grading card to the desired outcome. This is
the structure the method asks for.

### Weaknesses

**The chosen job is close to a description of today's tools.**
"Teachers need to assign work to students, then provide feedback and grades"
names the activities Canvas and Gradescope already perform. The desired
outcome ("students can easily see how they're doing") is the student's,
while the job is written for the teacher. A job one level up, such as knowing
whether students are learning and helping them improve, would test whether
grades are the right output at all. As written, Opp 3's "live standing for
students" meets the outcome without asking that question.

**The opportunities overlap features the slide already lists.**
Slide 2 names Gradescope as the tool for rubric grading and regrade requests,
and a shared rubric with a comment bank is part of what it offers today.
Questions pinned to an assignment are also common in course forums. This is
not a request for market proof. The team's own framing points somewhere
sharper: if the tools are split across the job, the gap may be between the
tools rather than inside any one of them.

**The opportunities don't follow the team's own ratings.**
Step 6 (decide how to grade, importance 4, satisfaction 2) is not carried
forward, while step 4 (do it and ask questions, 4/3) is. The ratings are the
team's estimates, and a student team is rating instructor satisfaction on
steps 6 and 7 without saying how. Slide 2 also opens with AI saving teachers
about 6 hours a week, and AI does not appear again in the map or the
opportunities.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the framing.**
   *"Your slide says the job is stable but the tools are split across it.
   Where in your eight steps does that split cause the most trouble, and how
   do you know?"*
   If they own it, they'll name a specific handoff (a question asked in Piazza
   about an assignment posted in Canvas and graded in Gradescope). If they
   don't, they'll restate the tool list.

2. **Move the job up one level.**
   *"If grades didn't exist, what would a teacher still be trying to get done
   in this job?"*
   Tests whether the team can separate the job from the activity. A strong
   answer arrives at something like "know who is learning and help them
   improve," and then asks whether live standing serves that.

3. **Check who did the rating.**
   *"You rated the teacher's satisfaction with deciding how to grade and with
   grading at 2. How did you arrive at a teacher's ratings, and why did step 6
   drop out of your opportunities?"*
   Tests assumption awareness, not evidence. A good answer says the ratings
   are estimates and names who they would ask first.

4. **Find the gap (use if team is strong).**
   *"Gradescope already offers rubrics, reusable comments, and regrade
   requests. What still goes wrong for a TA grading a few hundred
   submissions?"*
   Separates "a tool exists" from "this specific problem remains," the same
   check used with the Week 4 Grid team.

## Team: Wearable Health Tracking

**Team:** Kendall Frederick, Sissi Lai, Owen Bartlett, Evan Batten (the cover
lists "Owen" and "Evan" without surnames)
**Domain:** Wearable health tracking, with "Should I see a doctor?" as the
mapped job
**Methodology:** Jobs to Be Done (user processes)
**Submitted:** Week 6 deck (week62), on time. Speaker notes on the
opportunities slide only.

### Summary

Team opened with the market: $103B in wearable technology in 2026, projected
to reach $231B by 2033; smart ring shipments up 53% while smartwatches dip 4%;
and more than 5 million paid Oura members. The "what's new" column names the
FDA's January 2026 wellness guidance, which lets wearables estimate blood
pressure and glucose without clearance, and states that "interpretation, not
sensors, is the new battleground." Slide 3 gives three jobs: functional (when
something feels off, get objective data to decide whether to see a doctor),
emotional (feel reassured and in control each morning, without worry over one
bad reading), and social (signal the right image for the setting). The
functional job is mapped in eight steps from noticing a symptom to follow-up,
with a gap column (importance minus satisfaction). The largest gaps are
briefing the doctor (+4), interpreting and deciding the next step (+3 each),
and capturing data (+2). Three opportunities are ranked by those gaps:
clinical handoff, insight to action, and frictionless wear. A design
constraint runs under all three: stay within FDA wellness guidance, surface
patterns, and never diagnose.

### Strengths

**Used all three kinds of job, and the social job is a real finding.**
A visible WHOOP strap signals "athlete" at practice, an Oura ring passes as
jewelry at work, and people swap bands or take the device off. That
observation feeds Opp 3, since a device taken off for social reasons captures
no data when symptoms arrive. This is the only deck this week to use the
emotional and social dimensions of the method.

**The opportunities are ranked by the team's own numbers.**
The gap column is computed for every step, and the notes order the
opportunities by it: clinical handoff (+4), insight to action (+3, +3),
frictionless wear (+2). The ranking follows from the analysis rather than
from which idea sounds best, which is new for the class.

**Treated regulation as a design constraint and checked its own figures.**
The notes contrast two paths through the FDA line (Apple sought clearance for
hypertension notifications; WHOOP launched a wellness feature and received a
warning letter), and every opportunity is written to stay on the wellness
side. The notes also warn against the "90% of health spending" figure, which
PolitiFact rated False, and label the ratings as hypotheses to validate with
interviews.

### Weaknesses

**The largest gap belongs to someone else's job.**
Briefing the doctor scores satisfaction 1 because, in the deck's words,
"visits are short and clinicians rarely have tools to use wearable data." The
barrier described sits with the clinician: a 15-minute visit, data that isn't
in the medical record, and no one assigned to review it. The map follows only
the patient. Opp 1 may need the clinician's job mapped as well, since a
one-page summary helps only if the doctor has a reason and the time to read
it. This is the Week 4 pattern of a constraint that sits in an institution
rather than a missing tool.

**Opportunity 2 sits close to the line the team draws.**
"Outside your range for 3 nights" is a sensible threshold that answers the
emotional job's concern about one bad reading. Suggesting that the user raise
it with a doctor, though, acts on the decide-next-step stage, which is the
part of the job the FDA line governs, and WHOOP's warning letter came for a
wellness feature. The deck doesn't say what the prompt can and cannot say, or
how false alarms would affect the reassurance the emotional job asks for.

**Opportunity 3 is where the market is already moving.**
The team's own slide 2 shows smart rings growing 53% and Oura holding roughly
three quarters of ring shipments, and charge-while-worn, discreet form factors
are what that category sells. The deck links the 30% abandonment figure to
charging and fit without showing why people abandon trackers; if they stop
because the data stopped being useful, the fix is Opp 2 rather than
hardware.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the largest gap.**
   *"Briefing the doctor has your biggest gap. Why is satisfaction a 1 there,
   and is that the patient's problem or the doctor's?"*
   With notes only on the last slide, this is the best check on ownership. A
   strong answer describes the visit from both sides. A weak one restates "the
   visit is short."

2. **Map the other side of the handoff.**
   *"Suppose a clinician gets your one-page summary tomorrow. What would they
   have to do with it, and what would make them ignore it?"*
   Moves the team toward the clinician's job without asking for a solution.
   A good answer names time, liability, or whether the data belongs in the
   record.

3. **Locate Opportunity 2 relative to the line.**
   *"WHOOP received a warning letter for a wellness feature. What exactly does
   'suggest raising it with a doctor' say that keeps Insight to Action on the
   wellness side?"*
   Tests whether the design constraint is understood or only stated.

4. **Check the abandonment link (use if time).**
   *"About 30% of trackers end up abandoned. Do people stop because of
   charging and fit, or because the data stopped being useful?"*
   A strong answer sees that the answer decides between Opp 3 and Opp 2.

## Team: Transport & Mobility -- Routing Algorithms

**Team:** Michael Squeri, Jingchen Jiang, Jonathan Locala, Sichen Deng (the
cover lists "Team 4: Michael, Jason, Jonathan, Sichen")
**Domain:** Transportation and mobility, with door-to-door travel as the job
**Methodology:** Jobs to Be Done
**Submitted:** Week 6 deck (week64), flagged LATE. The ratings on slide 3 are
shown as dots only; they were read from the slide for this briefing.

### Summary

Team restated its job to take the solution out of it: the first phrasing,
"transport with efficiency" with scooters and Google Maps, became getting
from where I am to where I need to be with as little time, cost, and effort
as possible, without excess emissions. Supporting data: 49 hours lost to
congestion per US driver in 2025 (about $894 each, $85.8B nationally), an
average transit wait of about 10 minutes, a five-minute transfer window that
holds only about 80% of the time, and Google Maps used by 70% of US
navigation-app users. A chart of average commute times (25 minutes driving
alone, 45 by bus, 47 by subway, 69 by commuter rail) illustrates that the gap
isn't explained by vehicle speed. Seven door-to-door steps are rated, with
gaps at waiting (importance 5, satisfaction 1 on the slide), transferring
(4/1), and parking or returning the vehicle (4/2). The main finding, in the
notes: "All three underserved steps sit between modes rather than inside one.
Every current solution owns a single mode, so nobody owns the handoffs." Four
problem spaces follow: know the curb before you arrive, the train everyone
knows is late, construction you can see coming, and routes that weigh more
than time. The first two act on the rated gaps; the notes present the other
two as supporting ideas.

### Strengths

**Took the solution out of the job, and said so.**
"That phrasing had the solution inside it." The team rewrote its own job
statement and changed what it measures as a result: elapsed door-to-door time
and predictability rather than vehicle speed, and footprint per person moved
rather than per vehicle. This is the clearest use of the method's first step
in the three decks.

**Found the week's sharpest insight: nobody owns the handoffs.**
Waiting, transferring, and parking all happen between modes, and every
current product (a map app, a transit agency, a scooter company) owns one
mode. The commute chart supports it: a bus is not half as fast as a car, so
the time is lost outside the ride. The finding explains why the steps stay
underserved rather than only reporting that they are.

**Ratings that discriminate, and careful with evidence.**
Boarding and paying rates well (importance 2, satisfaction 4), which the
notes use to show the ratings aren't uniformly negative. The notes caveat the
parking-cruising figure as downtown-only, call the commute averages an
illustration rather than a controlled comparison, and warn that routing
around "unsafe" areas has been criticized as digital redlining, so safety
should be defined by road-level evidence.

### Weaknesses

**The handoff finding doesn't carry into the opportunities.**
The closing line names the common fault as routing that "runs on what
already happened, reports the scheduled world, stops at the address, and
optimizes one number." That is a description of the routing layer, and the
deck's title is "Routing Algorithms." The job was cleaned of its solution on
slide 3, and the solution returned on slide 4. Only the late-train card
touches a transfer, and it tells riders a connection is unreliable rather
than making the connection hold. None of the four says who would own a
handoff.

**Half of the cards act outside the rated gaps.**
The construction card acts "before you drive," which the notes put outside
the mapped scope (leaving the origin to arriving). The routes card acts on
the ride step. On the slide's dots, riding rates importance 5 and
satisfaction 3, the same +2 gap as parking, yet parking is marked as a gap
and riding is not. The rule for what counts as a gap isn't stated.

**Some claims are broader than the deck needs, and two standards already
exist.**
"These simple algorithms have largely remained unchanged for decades"
understates navigation apps that already use live and predicted traffic; the
deck's narrower point (they optimize one number) holds without it. The notes
also report that the Curb Data Specification exists and that the Work Zone
Data Exchange has fed navigation providers since 2020, with only 40 feeds
active. That suggests the constraint is who publishes the data, which is an
institutional question, not an algorithm.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the key finding.**
   *"You say nobody owns the handoffs. Who would have to own a transfer for it
   to hold, and why doesn't anyone today?"*
   If they own it, they'll talk about separate operators, schedules, and
   who pays when a connection fails. If they don't, they'll restate that the
   steps are between modes.

2. **Connect the finding to the cards.**
   *"Your main finding is about handoffs between modes. Which of your four
   cards would make a transfer more likely to hold, not just easier to
   predict?"*
   Tests whether the team sees that its opportunities moved back to the
   routing layer. A strong team will propose a handoff problem of its own.

3. **Find the constraint.**
   *"The curb data standard and the work zone feed both already exist, and
   only 40 work zone feeds are active. Is the problem the routing algorithm,
   or who publishes the data?"*
   The same check the Week 4 Construction team made with Katerra: better
   tools, with the constraint one level up.

4. **State the gap rule (use if team is strong).**
   *"On your slide, riding rates 5 and 3, the same gap as parking. Why is
   parking a gap and riding not?"*
   Checks whether the ratings decided the focus or the focus decided the
   ratings.

## Team: Cold Chain Monitoring

**Team:** Sophia Mai, Sahana Raja, Daniel Sung, Kerry Wang (Canvas group
"Week 6 3"; no names on any slide, and the notes refer to "the author")
**Domain:** Food systems and agriculture technology, with refrigerated
shipments as the job
**Methodology:** Jobs to Be Done and User Process mapping
**Submitted:** Week 6 deck ("CSIE Week 6.pptx"), received Oct 19. The
filename has no Canvas prefix, so the submitter and on-time status can't be
read from it.

### Summary

Team set the scale first: about 14% of food is lost between harvest and
retail (FAO 2019), an estimated 526 million tonnes, about 12% of global food
production, was lost in 2017 to inadequate cold-chain capacity (FAO/UNEP),
and US supermarkets lose 11.6% of fresh vegetables (USDA ERS). The notes warn
that the two FAO figures measure different things and should not be added.
Slide 3 splits the response to a temperature problem into four steps (detect,
communicate, decide, act) and argues that today's technology is strongest at
the first two: connected sensors (Sensitech, Tive) stream data and alert in
real time, and Sensitech runs corrective-action programs with 18 of the top
20 global food-service and retail companies, which the team reads as evidence
the industry already sees a gap between detecting and resolving. The job is
written for a cold-chain logistics manager at a shipper, 3PL, or regional
distributor, with functional, emotional ("avoid the stress of 2 a.m. calls
with unclear authority to act"), and social needs. A nine-step process map is
rated 1 to 5, labeled as hypotheses: monitoring in transit rates 5/5, while
notifying and assigning responsibility and authorizing corrective action both
rate 5/2. Three opportunities follow, with the first marked PRIMARY on the
slide: an alert without an owner, product-specific interpretation of
excursions, and cross-boundary authority for corrective action. Slide 9 tests
the primary one as Real, Important, and Addressable.

### Strengths

**"An alert without an owner": the job separated from the tool.**
"The job is NOT 'monitor temperature.'" The notes restate it as delivering
acceptable food while protecting the people and relationships around that
delivery, and the deck finds that the tool for the old job (sensing) is
mature while the step after it is not. The four-step detect, communicate,
decide, act chain makes the argument visible in one line.

**Used a vendor's behavior as evidence of the gap.**
A major sensor vendor has built a whole service line around turning alerts
into corrective action. The team reads that as a market signal that the
bottleneck moved from detection to response, and uses it again on slide 9 as
evidence of addressability. This is a sharper kind of evidence than a market
size.

**The most careful handling of its own claims this week.**
The ratings are labeled hypotheses on the slide, the 2 a.m. scenario is
labeled illustrative, the notes flag that the strongest numbers come from
pharma rather than food, and the deck adds a nuance against its own title:
FSMA and contracts already assign formal responsibility, so the gap is
between formal accountability and practical coordination in the moment. Each
opportunity ends with "Evidence still needed."

### Weaknesses

**The primary opportunity may be the symptom of the third.**
An alert has no owner at 2 a.m. because authority to re-route, re-ice, or
reject a load was never agreed across shipper, carrier, and receiver, which
is Opportunity 3. Slide 9 says as much ("whether a workable intervention
changes contractual incentives"). If the constraint is in the contract, it
sits one level above the alert, and better routing of the alert would not
change who is willing to act. This is the Week 4 Katerra lesson in a new
domain.

**The core evidence comes from companies that sell the fix.**
The 20% of shipments arriving compromised and the $35 billion figure come
from Tive, and the 60% faster response comes from a Navixy case; both sell
incident-response tools, and both figures describe pharma or general cold
chain. The team flags the pharma limit but not the sellers' interest. This
is an assumption-awareness point, not a request for proof.

**It doesn't say who is left once the large players are served.**
If Sensitech already runs corrective-action programs with 18 of the top 20
food retailers, the gap for the largest shippers may already be paid for. The
primary user includes regional distributors, and slide 9 says the loss falls
hardest on growers and receivers least able to absorb it. That smaller
segment may be the real opportunity, and the deck doesn't name it.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the scenario.**
   *"Walk us through the 2 a.m. temperature rise: who gets the alert, who
   has authority to decide, and what would have had to be agreed before the
   truck left?"*
   With no names on the deck, this is also the best check on who did the
   work. Consider asking two different members.

2. **Symptom or cause.**
   *"If authority were agreed in the contract before every shipment, which
   is your third opportunity, would the alert-without-an-owner problem still
   exist?"*
   Tests whether the team sees that its constraint may sit in the contract.
   A strong answer says what part of the problem would remain.

3. **Find who is left.**
   *"Sensitech already runs corrective-action programs with 18 of the top
   20 food retailers. Whose alerts still don't reach anyone?"*
   Moves the team toward a specific segment (regional distributors, growers)
   without asking for a solution.

4. **Check the sources (use if time).**
   *"Your strongest numbers come from Tive and Navixy, which sell response
   tools, and from pharma. What would you expect a produce receiver to tell
   you?"*
   Assumption awareness about who produced the evidence.

## Cross-Student Intelligence

**Four domains, one finding: the largest gap sits at a handoff nobody
owns.**
[EdTech] "The job is stable but the tools are split across it" (Canvas,
Gradescope, Piazza). [Wearable] The largest gap (+4) is briefing the doctor,
where the patient's data has to cross to a clinician who has no tools for it.
[Transport] All three underserved steps sit between modes, and "nobody owns
the handoffs." [Cold Chain] Detection is mature, and the gap is "an alert
without an owner" as a problem crosses from carrier to shipper to receiver.
Each team mapped a different job and found its worst step at a boundary
between tools, people, or companies. The teams propose different fixes.
EdTech consolidates: one home for questions, tied to the work. Wearable
builds a better handoff: a summary that crosses to the doctor, who decides.
Cold Chain points to agreeing in advance who may act, so the handoff has an
owner before the alert fires. Transport improves information inside one tool
(the routing app) and leaves the handoff unowned. This extends Week 4
Construction's fragmented workflows ("no single party accountable for the
outcome") and Week 2 Smart Cities' chain of custody at every handoff.
**Recommend presenting EdTech, Wearable, and Cold Chain back-to-back and
asking the room: should the fix be one tool that owns the whole job, a better
handoff between parties that stay separate, or authority agreed before
anything goes wrong?**

**Generic thresholds and personal baselines.**
[Cold Chain, Opp 2] says one generic temperature threshold creates false
alarms for hardy produce and misses risk for dairy or seafood. [Wearable, Opp
2] says population ranges leave users unable to tell what is normal for them,
and proposes flagging a pattern against the person's own baseline. Both are
asking for an alert judged against the specific thing being measured. Worth
asking each team the other's question: what does a false alarm cost, and who
stops listening?

**A live score can reassure or alarm.**
[EdTech, Opp 3] proposes "live standing for students" as the way to meet its
desired outcome. [Wearable] found that people checking readiness scores each
morning want to feel reassured, "without worry over one bad reading," and
designed its pattern alerts around three nights rather than one. Worth asking
the EdTech team the Wearable team's question: what does a student feel when a
live grade drops after one assignment?

**Cheaper grading and what gets assigned.**
[EdTech, Opp 3] (fast, consistent grading) meets Week 4 Curriculum's "what's
easy to grade gets taught." If grading gets faster, the capacity could go to
work that is harder to grade, or the same easy-to-grade work could simply get
cheaper. Both decks describe the tools and grading load of courses like this
one (Canvas, Gradescope, TAs). As in Week 4, treat it as useful input and
keep the questions open rather than defensive.

[Transport, know the curb] and Week 2 Smart Cities' logistics real-estate
exchange (pricing curb space); Jonathan and Sichen were on that team.
[Transport, routes that weigh more than time] and Week 2 Social Media's

## Briefing Design Notes

This briefing is calibrated for the opportunity-scanning phase (Weeks 2-10).
The weaknesses above focus on framing quality, abstraction level, how the job
is separated from the solution, and assumption awareness, not evidence or
market proof. Validation-level scrutiny is for Weeks 12-14.

**Method pattern this week: does the opportunity serve the job, or today's
tool?** All four teams mapped a job and rated its steps, and the common gap
is the step from the map back to the opportunity. Transport and Cold Chain
are the examples to point to for separating the job from the solution, and
both say so ("that phrasing had the solution inside it"; "the job is NOT
'monitor temperature'"), although Transport's title and four cards return to
the routing layer. Wearable and Cold Chain are the examples for following
their own ratings into their opportunities, and Cold Chain also tests its
primary opportunity as Real, Important, and Addressable. EdTech's job
statement stays close to what today's tools perform, and its choice of steps
doesn't follow its ratings. All four use the team's own estimates for the
ratings, which is acceptable for scanning; Wearable and Cold Chain label them
as hypotheses to check.

**Tracking from Weeks 2-5:**
- *Picking one opportunity.* Cold Chain marks its primary opportunity on the
  slide, and Wearable ranks all three by gap, with clinical handoff first,
  which is the first ranking in the class derived from the method's own
  numbers. Transport separates two primary cards from two supporting ones.
  EdTech has no pick.
- *Problems vs. solutions.* Cold Chain ("starting hypotheses ... not finished
  product proposals") and Transport ("problem spaces, not product pitches")
  state problems, and Wearable separates Problem from Idea. EdTech's cards
  end in feature lists ("Rubric with a comment bank").
- *Corrective forces.* Cold Chain argues against its own title (FSMA and
  contracts already assign formal responsibility) and flags that its numbers
  come from pharma. Wearable's regulatory line (the WHOOP warning letter) and
  Transport's digital-redlining caution and permit-date caveat are also
  forces the teams raised against their own ideas. EdTech raises none.
- *Business-model thinking.* Lighter than Week 5, where the Feed Is the
  Newsroom team named a payer for every opportunity, and still expected at
  this stage. Cold Chain comes closest, reading Sensitech's corrective-action
  programs as proof the market already pays for this gap. Wearable's $2.2
  trillion is the size of the problem, not a payer. Who pays for the
  clinician's time, and who pays a city to publish its curb rules, are both
  open and worth revisiting at the portfolio review.
