# Faculty Briefing -- Week 4

*Monday-morning briefing, one card per team, generated from the four Week 4
domain decks. All four teams applied Nested Hierarchies (systems analysis):
map the levels above and below a focal point, find the level where the
constraint sits, and choose what kind of innovation to make there.
Calibrated for the opportunity-scanning phase (Weeks 2-10). The weaknesses
below are about framing quality, abstraction level, where the constraint is
placed, and assumption awareness, not evidence or market proof. Validation
scrutiny is for Weeks 12-14.*

## Team: Disappearing Junior-Level Software Jobs

**Team:** Not listed on any slide. Submitted under Owen Bartlett's Canvas ID;
by elimination from the roster, likely Owen Bartlett, Jingchen Jiang,
Jonathan Locala, Sophia Mai, and Daniel Sung (confirm).
**Domain:** The software talent system, with the junior engineer role as
focal point
**Methodology:** Nested Hierarchies
**Submitted:** Week 4 deck (week41), on time

### Summary

Team framed the software talent system around the junior engineer role, the
step where beginners become experienced engineers. New-grad hiring at the 12
largest tech companies is down 65% from 2019 and recent CS graduates face
6.1% unemployment, while BLS projects software developer employment to grow
16% through 2034. The speaker notes separate the two trends: engineering
hiring fell about 11% while new-grad hiring fell 65-76%. The hierarchy runs
from macro economy and policy, through the talent ecosystem and the firm, down
to the junior role, which they split into production, learning, and signaling
sub-systems. They name learning as the reverse salient: experience used to
come free with routine work, and AI made routine work cheap. Four
opportunities are ordered around the hiring gate (before, at, after, and
re-imagining the system): new pathways for building experience, a greater
value on engineering judgment, new junior roles in AI-enabled teams, and
industry-level talent investment. No strongest pick is named; the notes lean
toward 03.

### Strengths

**The unbundling insight.**
"The junior role bundled production and learning; AI unbundled them." The
notes explain that a junior used to pay for their own training through
routine work, and once AI took that work "the cross-subsidy disappeared."
This accounts for why the drop is concentrated at entry level rather than
across engineering, and the 11% vs. 65-76% split supports it. The team
changed the question from "are juniors skilled enough" to "what was paying
for their training."

**Found a constraint above the focal point.**
Training is an industry-level externality: a firm that develops a junior may
watch a competitor hire them away. The team looked up the hierarchy as the
method asks, and the "self-undermining loop" (firms now prize judgment, which
the junior years used to build) follows from it.

**Considered a future that argues against them.**
The "rebound scenario" (AI-fluent juniors reach mid-level faster, so hiring
them pays off again) is a corrective force the team raised itself. Week 2
decks were flagged for leaving these out.

### Weaknesses

**The opportunities read as forecasts rather than problems.**
Each one ends in a prediction: experience "may become separable" from
employment, judgment "may become a stronger differentiator," developing
engineers "becomes increasingly valuable." None names who has the problem
today or what currently fails for them. The construction deck's WHO / WHY /
SIGNAL lines are a useful comparison.

**The externality diagnosis is not carried into the opportunities.**
If firms under-invest in training because they cannot keep the benefit, then
Opp 01 (pathways outside employment) and Opp 04 (industry talent investment)
both need a payer who can. The notes compare Opp 04 to medical residency, but
residency is largely publicly funded (Medicare is the largest single funder of
residency positions), and that is the part the analogy depends on.

**Opportunities 02 and 03 assume demand the deck doesn't show.**
Opp 02's signal (84% use AI, 46% distrust its accuracy) comes from a developer
survey; it shows developers distrust AI output, not that employers hire or pay
for judgment. Opp 03 assumes a firm will place a junior between a senior and
the AI agents, when slide 4 shows the firm already getting the output from a
senior plus AI.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the key reframe.**
   *"You say AI didn't make juniors less skilled; it removed what used to pay
   for their training. Walk us through how you got from the hiring numbers to
   that conclusion."*
   If they own it, they'll explain the 11% vs. 65-76% split and the
   cross-subsidy without the slide. If they don't, they'll restate "AI took
   the routine work."

2. **Challenge the analogy.**
   *"Your notes compare industry talent investment to medical residency.
   Residency works partly because Medicare pays for much of it. Who plays
   Medicare for software engineering?"*
   Tests whether they understand how the residency model works or just liked
   the parallel. A strong answer connects back to their own externality point.

3. **Test the demand behind Opportunity 03.**
   *"Slide 4 shows one senior plus AI agents getting the output. Why would a
   firm put a junior between the senior and the agents?"*
   A strong answer uses their experience-lag point (today's missing juniors
   are tomorrow's senior gap) and says who notices that cost first. A weak one
   restates that juniors could manage AI.

4. **Turn a forecast into a problem (use if time).**
   *"Pick one opportunity and tell us who has this problem today and what
   they're doing about it now."*
   Moves the opportunities from predictions toward problems someone currently
   has, without asking for a solution.

## Team: Construction Inefficiencies

**Team:** Sissi Lai, Yash Chadda, Flora Huang, Steven Li, Evan Batten
**Domain:** Construction productivity and project delivery
**Methodology:** Nested Hierarchies
**Submitted:** Week 4 deck (week42), on time

### Summary

Team opened with the gap between better tools and flat output: construction
productivity has grown about 1% a year for two decades while machines,
materials, and software improved sharply, and large projects run roughly 80%
over budget and 20% late. They treated labor as a replacement problem rather
than a growth problem (349,000 net new workers needed in 2026 per ABC, with
41% of the workforce projected to retire by 2031). The hierarchy has five
levels with the project as focal point and the constraint one level above it,
in project delivery (design, permits, contracts), where every building is "a
one-off prototype." Katerra is the test case: more than $2B bought factories
and an integrated value chain (levels 1-2), and the company went bankrupt in
2021 on jurisdictional code variation and contracting relationships. They
produced three problems: every project starts from scratch, fragmented
workflows with no party accountable for the outcome, and experience leaving
with a retiring workforce. The speaker notes name 02 as most addressable.

### Strengths

**Katerra as a natural experiment for the method.**
"Two billion dollars bought a complete Level 1 and 2 solution. It failed on
contracting relationships and jurisdictional code variation." The team used a
real failure to test where the constraint sits instead of asserting it. This
is the strongest use of the nested-hierarchies tool of the four decks.

**Reasoned from where improvement already happened.**
Level 1 was transformed and productivity did not move, so the constraint must
be elsewhere. This applies the worksheet's "a better motor does not help"
logic to evidence, and it explains why the team set aside its first instinct
(tools).

**Careful with numbers.**
The notes lead with ABC's conservative 349,000 and hold Deloitte's 499,000 as
the higher estimate, and labor demand is identified as retirement-driven. Each
problem carries WHO / WHY / SIGNAL lines.

### Weaknesses

**Opportunity 03 is not a level 4 problem.**
The slide is titled "Opportunities at level 4" and closes with "Every reason
those fixes fail sits at level 4," but the retiring-workforce problem sits at
level 2 (crews) and is a separate story from the productivity gap the deck is
built on. It may still be a good opportunity; the connection to the
constraint is not drawn.

**Level 5 is named and then set aside.**
The Katerra slide says what stopped the company sat at "levels 4 and 5," and
the notes say level 4. Jurisdictional code variation and who is allowed to
build arguably belong to level 5 (government and planning). If Opp 01's
repetition requires more uniform codes, the constraint may sit higher than
the team placed it, which changes who could act on it.

**Opportunity 02 may already have a contract form.**
The notes say integrated delivery contracts "already exist in other sectors."
Design-build and integrated project delivery also exist within construction
itself. That does not make the problem solved, but it changes the question
from "no one has the contract" to "why hasn't the existing contract fixed
it."

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the key argument.**
   *"Katerra spent $2B and failed. How do you know it failed because of level 4
   and not because of how it was run?"*
   If they own it, they'll point to the specific evidence (state-by-state
   codes, contractors refusing to leave their subcontractors, the factory never
   activated). A strong team will also admit that one case cannot rule out
   execution.

2. **Challenge the "most addressable" pick.**
   *"Design-build and integrated project delivery contracts already exist in
   construction. If the contract exists, why are large projects still running
   80% over budget?"*
   Tests whether 02 is a contracting problem, as the notes claim, or a problem
   of incentives and accountability that the contract doesn't reach.

3. **Locate Opportunity 03.**
   *"Your slide says every reason the fixes fail sits at level 4. Where does the
   retiring workforce sit, and how does it connect to your constraint?"*
   A good answer either connects it (for example, if nothing repeats, every
   project leans harder on experienced judgment) or acknowledges it as a
   separate opportunity.

4. **Look up one more level (use if team is strong).**
   *"If building codes differ by jurisdiction, can anyone at level 4 make
   repetition possible without level 5 changing?"*
   Shows whether they chose level 4 because that's where the constraint is or
   because it's more addressable than government.

## Team: Grid Modernization

**Team:** Sylvia Yam, Kendall Frederick, Penn Riney, Sichen Deng
**Domain:** Electricity grid resilience and distributed energy, with BGE's
service territory as focal point
**Methodology:** Nested Hierarchies
**Submitted:** Week 4 deck (week43), on time. No speaker notes; the hierarchy
on slide 4 is an image and was read for this briefing.

### Summary

Team opened with Texas in February 2021: more than 4.5 million people lost
power, 20,000 MW of rolling blackouts, and only 820 MW of links to the rest of
the US grid, enough to cover about 4% of the shortfall. They framed the grid
as built one-way (plants to passive users) while solar, batteries, and EVs now
send power back, citing 33 GW of virtual power plants today and a DOE target
of 80-160 GW by 2030. The hierarchy uses BGE's service territory as focal
point, with PJM/the Eastern Interconnection and the US energy system above,
and the local distribution grid and devices below. The reverse salient is the
distribution grid (level 2), where "risk flows down" and "change flows up."
The team changes one element: each neighborhood becomes a cell that can
island and share power over controllable DC links, coordinated by software.
Three opportunities follow: last-mile visibility behind the meter, virtual
power plant orchestration, and a routing protocol for power. No strongest
pick is named.

### Strengths

**A clear two-direction reading of the hierarchy.**
"Risk flows down" (an upstream failure darkens every neighborhood) and
"change flows up" (devices now send power back) meet at level 2. This is a
precise explanation of why the distribution grid is the reverse salient, and
the local focal point grounds the analysis in a system the team can observe.

**Opportunities are sequenced, not only listed.**
The "what cascades" column states that the other two ideas depend on
last-mile visibility. That dependency is a step toward choosing where to
start.

**Named the risk in its own proposal.**
"More software on the grid means a larger attack surface." The team raised
the second-order cost of its design without prompting.

### Weaknesses

**The opening case argues for connection, and the proposal adds islanding.**
Texas failed because it was isolated and could not import power, and 76% of
outages came from frozen plants and gas supply, which sit at level 5. The
proposal makes each neighborhood able to isolate itself. The DC links between
cells may answer this, but the deck does not connect the Texas lesson to
level 2, and the case comes from ERCOT rather than BGE/PJM.

**"Institutional inertia" is named as what's stuck, but all three
opportunities are software.**
The level 2 slide lists institutional inertia and slow infrastructure
replacement as what is stuck. Peaks being "met by building plants while home
batteries and EVs sit idle" may reflect how utilities are regulated and paid
(a return on capital investment) rather than a missing tool. The construction
team's Katerra argument, better tools with the constraint one level up,
applies here.

**The opportunities overlap existing product categories.**
The team's own slide reports 33 GW of virtual power plants already operating,
and utilities already run distributed-energy management systems aimed at
behind-the-meter visibility. This is not a request for market proof. Asking
what existing tools miss would sharpen the problem (the same note made for
the Week 3 cybersecurity team).

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Reconcile the case and the proposal.**
   *"Texas went dark because it couldn't import power. Your design lets every
   neighborhood cut itself off. Walk us through why that helps rather than
   hurts."*
   If they own the design, they'll explain islanding as a fault boundary and
   the DC links as the import path. If they don't, they'll restate the slide.
   With no speaker notes, this is also the best check on ownership.

2. **Find the real constraint.**
   *"You list institutional inertia as what's stuck. If a utility earns its
   return by building plants, does better software change what it chooses to
   do?"*
   Tests whether the opportunities sit at the level of the constraint. A strong
   team will name who would need to change (the regulator, rate design) or
   argue why software alone shifts the incentive.

3. **Find the gap.**
   *"Your slide says 33 GW of virtual power plants already exist. What isn't
   working about them that your orchestration opportunity would fix?"*
   Separates "this category exists" from "this specific problem remains."

4. **Force a choice (use if team is strong).**
   *"You say the other two ideas depend on last-mile visibility. Is that the
   one you'd carry into your portfolio, and who at BGE would you talk to
   first?"*
   No pick appears in the deck.

## Team: Curriculum as Part of a System

**Team:** Michael Squeri, Sahana Raja, Kerry Wang
**Domain:** Higher education, with the curriculum as focal point
**Methodology:** Systems Analysis (Nested Hierarchies)
**Submitted:** Week 4 deck (week44), on time

### Summary

Team took higher education as the domain and the curriculum as focal point,
in a hierarchy of elements, classes, curriculum, education, and workforce.
The framing is a mismatch of clock speeds: 39% of workers' core skills are
expected to change by 2030, while a substantive curriculum change takes about
two years to reach the catalog. Supporting data: 88% of students and 77% of
faculty use AI, under 30% of students think their assessments reflect an
AI-enabled workplace, and Harvard grades have compressed toward A's. Six
opportunity spaces are written as "How might we" questions and ordered by
innovation level (elements, interactions, system, purpose): what's easy to
grade gets taught, the skills nobody assigns, the transcript stopped
signaling, theory without application, a degree that stops at 22, and why pay
for the degree. No strongest pick is named.

### Strengths

**"What's easy to grade gets taught."**
Grading capacity (300 students, three TAs, autograders) quietly sets the
ceiling on what can be assigned, and the return to handwritten exams after
AI was chosen for gradeability rather than for what those exams measure. This
is a structural explanation the team found inside its own experience.

**The clock-speed framing.**
Placing 39% of skills changing by 2030 next to a two-year approval cycle
states the problem as a mismatch between levels of one system, not as bad
teaching or lazy students. The notes say this directly.

**Problems, not solutions, ordered by level.**
Each card states a problem and turns it into a question, and the notes add
three tests any solution would have to pass (minimum theory, where AI fits,
how we know it stuck). The purpose-level cards question what the system is
for.

### Weaknesses

**The constraint is placed in three different spots.**
The analysis slide puts it at the focal level ("the gap between what a course
could be and how it is actually taught"). The notes say much of what holds the
curriculum still is authorized one level above, in approval cycles and
credit-hour rules. The System card calls the two-year approval cycle "the
reverse salient." The method asks the team to commit to one level, and that
choice decides who could act.

**Six opportunities, no pick, and one answer built into a question.**
The Week 2 pattern of presenting a menu returns, and this is the largest one
so far. "How might a course cover essential theory in 2-3 weeks, then spend
the rest applying it" prescribes the solution inside the question; the
problem underneath is that application gets crowded out.

**Some evidence is stretched or mismatched.**
The notes describe a chart of the share of A grades (about 25% in 2005 to over
60%), but the slide image shows Harvard median GPA from 1950 to 2025. The
single-institution caveat appears only in the notes. "Palantir now hires
straight out of high school" rests on a 22-person fellowship, and the
two-year approval figure comes from two universities. That is acceptable for
scanning, but the slide claims are broader than the sources.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the key insight.**
   *"You say what's easy to grade gets taught. Give us an example from a course
   you've taken where grading capacity decided what was assigned."*
   If they own it, they'll name a specific course and assignment. If they
   don't, they'll repeat the 300-students example from the notes.

2. **Commit to a level.**
   *"Your analysis says the constraint is in how courses are taught. Your notes
   say it's the approval cycle one level up. Which is it, and who would have to
   act?"*
   Tests whether they understand that the location of the constraint decides
   who can act on it.

3. **Find the problem under the solution.**
   *"'Theory in 2-3 weeks' is an answer. What problem does it solve, stated
   without the solution?"*
   The same check used with the Week 3 Watchers team.

4. **Force a choice (use if team is strong).**
   *"Of your six, which one would you carry into your portfolio, and why that
   one over 'Why pay for the degree?'"*
   Reveals which opportunity the team believes in versus which sounds most
   ambitious.

## Cross-Student Intelligence

**Three domains, one problem: the way novices become experts is breaking.**
[Junior Jobs] Experience was a by-product of routine work, and AI now does
the routine work. [Construction, Opp 03] Tradespeople are retiring faster
than skills can be transferred, and supervision thins while new workers come
up. [Curriculum: "A degree that stops at 22," "The transcript stopped
signaling"] About 70% of employers say new graduates still need substantial
training, and grades no longer tell them who is ready. Both labor-focused
decks cite AI taking entry-level tasks first (SignalFire; Stanford Digital
Economy Lab). The two CS-adjacent teams also place responsibility in
different places. Junior Jobs puts it on industry: experience as shared
infrastructure, built outside traditional employment. Curriculum puts it on
the degree: application inside courses, and a degree that continues past
graduation. **Recommend presenting these two back-to-back and asking the room
who should pay for experience: the employer, the school, or someone else.**

**Better tools, stuck institutions: construction and the grid.**
[Construction] argues that level 1 improved for twenty years while the
constraint sat in delivery, contracts, and codes, and uses Katerra to show
what happens when money goes to the wrong level. [Grid] names institutional
inertia as what is stuck and then proposes three software opportunities.
Both are regulated, jurisdiction-fragmented infrastructure industries. Worth
asking the grid team the construction team's question: what would Katerra's
lesson say about a software fix for the grid?

**Two teams arrive at "judgment," which is where the course design also
lands.**
[Junior Jobs, Opp 02] "Engineering judgment may become a stronger
differentiator" and [Curriculum, "The skills nobody assigns"] "judgment and
communication" reach the same conclusion as the curriculum redesign's
Employer Signal ("The scarce resource is judgment"). Worth checking in the
ownership questions whether the teams derived this or absorbed it from course
framing. Separately, the Curriculum deck describes the course model being
taught ("theory in 2-3 weeks, then application"; "what's easy to grade gets
taught"). Treat it as useful input, and keep the questions open rather than
defensive.

**Threads carried over from Weeks 2-3.**
AI and work continues as the largest thread (Week 2 Public Approval of AI and
AI-generated media; Week 3 AI agents; this week two AI-and-labor decks).
[Grid] "more software on the grid means a larger attack surface" connects to
the Week 3 cybersecurity team's "the kill switch becomes the target." [Grid]
the isolated Texas interconnection and the Week 3 supply-chain chokepoints
both describe resilience lost to a single point of failure.

## Briefing Design Notes

This briefing is calibrated for the opportunity-scanning phase (Weeks 2-10).
The weaknesses above focus on framing quality, abstraction level, where the
constraint is placed, and assumption awareness, not evidence or market proof.
Validation-level scrutiny is for Weeks 12-14.

**Method pattern this week: does the opportunity sit at the level of the
constraint?** All four teams used nested hierarchies, and the common gap is
the step from locating a constraint to acting on it. Construction is the
example to point to: it places the constraint above the focal point, tests
the placement with Katerra, and keeps two of three opportunities at that
level. Grid names an institutional constraint and proposes software at level
2. Curriculum places the constraint in three different spots. Junior Jobs
finds constraints both below (the learning sub-system) and above (the
training externality), but its opportunities don't say who acts on the
externality.

**Tracking from Weeks 2-3:**
- *Picking one opportunity.* Only Construction names one, in the speaker
  notes, using the same prepared wording seen in Week 3 ("If asked which is
  most addressable, say 02"). Junior Jobs leans toward 03 in its notes. Grid
  and Curriculum have no pick, and Curriculum's six cards are the largest menu
  so far.
- *Problems vs. solutions.* Construction and Curriculum state "problems, not
  solutions," and Grid's table has a "The problem" column. Junior Jobs'
  opportunities drifted toward forecasts.
- *Corrective forces.* Junior Jobs' rebound scenario is the first deck to
  raise a force against its own thesis, which is progress on the Week 2
  "conveniently dark" critique.
- *Business-model thinking.* Still largely absent, which is expected. The
  closest to naming a payer are Construction's WHO lines (owners, general
  contractors, insurers) and Grid (utilities). Both labor decks leave "who
  pays for training" open, which is worth revisiting at the portfolio review.
