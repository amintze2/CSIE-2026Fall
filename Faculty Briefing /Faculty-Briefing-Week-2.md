## Team: Smart Cities / Rapid Delivery

**Team:** not captured from deck (names did not extract from slide/notes
text -- confirm roster)
**Domain:** Automated last-mile delivery / instant fulfillment
**Methodology:** Trending to the Absurd
**Submitted:** Week 2 deck (week21)

### Summary

Team took quick commerce (10-30 min delivery) as a real, growing domain and
carried it to an extreme where anything physical reaches you within 10
minutes -- ownership matters less than access, goods move before explicit
orders, and homes/curbs/rooftops become logistics network nodes. Produced
four opportunities: a curb/rooftop/skylane market exchange, a
trust-and-verification layer, reusable packaging/reverse logistics, and
peer-to-peer neighborhood fulfillment.

### Strengths

**Evidence discipline.**
Every claim is sourced -- Census e-commerce data, Amazon's same/next-day
volume, Walmart's drone-delivery stats, DoorDash's FAA certification. This
is the best-grounded domain background of the four teams.

**The infrastructure reframe.**
The team didn't stop at "delivery gets faster" -- they pushed to "delivery
becomes city infrastructure," which is what opened up governance-shaped
opportunities (curb space as a market, trust layers) instead of just
logistics-startup ideas.

### Weaknesses

**Four opportunities, no pick.**
Unlike a team that ranks and defends one opportunity, this team presented
all four at equal weight. That's a menu, not a bet -- worth pushing them to
choose before Week 3.

**Absurd world is conveniently frictionless.**
The extreme assumes near-instant fulfillment scales smoothly to "anything,
anywhere, 10 minutes" with no mention of which corrective forces (zoning
pushback, insurance costs, drone-safety regulation) might cap it well short
of that. Worth asking which opportunities survive if the extreme is
softened.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Force a choice.**
   *"Of your four opportunities, which one would you personally bet the
   semester on -- and why not the other three?"*
   Reveals whether they have a real conviction under the menu, or produced
   four options because they hadn't decided.

2. **Test the extreme's limits.**
   *"What's the first corrective force that would slow this down before it
   reaches your absurd extreme -- and does your opportunity survive it?"*
   Tests whether the team understands their own extreme is a chosen
   simplification, not a prediction.

## Team: Public Approval of AI

**Team:** Sissi, Kerry, Penn, Evan
**Domain:** Public trust/approval of AI vs. AI adoption
**Methodology:** Trending to the Absurd
**Submitted:** Week 2 deck (week22)

### Summary

Team identified a divergence trend: AI use keeps expanding (73% have used
an AI tool) while approval does not follow (52% more concerned than
excited, up from 37% in 2021). Framed via an S-curve -- AI moving from
optional tool to embedded infrastructure -- and carried it to an extreme
where AI becomes essential infrastructure before legitimacy catches up.
Produced and *ranked* three opportunities: a junior-to-senior talent
pipeline collapse, an AI-accountability tracker, and the cost-socialization
of data-center power -- naming the last as strongest.

### Strengths

**They defended a pick.**
This is the only team that ranked its opportunities and argued for one:
"the cost is already itemized on a bill people open every month." That's
the real/important/addressable case made concrete, not just asserted.

**Global framing, not just US.**
Brought a 25-country data point (34% more concerned than excited, no
country leading on excitement) that keeps the domain from reading as a
US-only anxiety.

### Weaknesses

**The dominant-design hypothesis is asserted, not tested.**
"AI becomes an embedded layer" is stated as the likely path but not argued
against alternatives (e.g., a backlash path where embedding triggers
opt-out demand, per the social-media team's domain this same week). Worth
asking what would falsify it.

**Opportunity 3's payer is still abstract.**
"Households in host regions" pay via rate increases, but no specific
customer or channel for a venture sits behind that yet -- strong problem
framing, thin on who buys a solution.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test ownership of the ranking.**
   *"Walk us through why you picked the power-cost opportunity over the
   other two. What would change your mind?"*
   If they own it, they'll reconstruct the reasoning live. If they don't,
   they'll just repeat the slide.

2. **Push toward a payer.**
   *"Who specifically writes a check to fix the power-cost problem -- a
   utility, a state regulator, a data-center operator?"*
   Forces the opportunity from "real and important" toward "addressable."

## Team: Group 3 -- Addictive and Opaque Social Media Algorithms

**Team:** Jason, Sahana, Sophia, Sylvia
**Domain:** Social media recommendation algorithms under legal/regulatory
pressure
**Methodology:** Trending to the Absurd
**Submitted:** Week 2 deck (week23)

### Summary

Team tracked a legal shift: courts and regulators are now treating
recommendation-system design itself (not content) as the harm -- the March
2026 LA jury verdict against Meta/Google, a $12B-$17B multistate
settlement, 3,000+ cases in federal MDL. Carried the trend to an extreme
where short-form ranking sets the political agenda and shapes children's
entire world-model. Produced two opportunities: user-set feed-optimization
goals, and an actionable/source-labeled layer on short-form clips -- both
explicitly scoped as buildable without platform cooperation.

### Strengths

**Most legally specific domain of the four.**
Dollar figures, case names, statute effective dates (NY SAFE for Kids,
Jan 2027; EU DSA) instead of survey trendlines. This domain is standing on
enforceable mechanisms, not just sentiment.

**Both opportunities pass "addressable" cleanly.**
Explicitly scoped as a browser extension / share-API layer needing no
platform buy-in for v1 -- the most concretely buildable pair of
opportunities across all four decks.

### Weaknesses

**"Open problems" outnumber opportunities.**
The team surfaced six cracks (opt-out feeds unused, transparency tools
that explain nothing, age verification unsolved, etc.) but only converted
two into opportunities. Worth asking why the other four weren't pursued --
too hard, not addressable yet, or just not chosen?

**The absurd extreme borrows its darkest framing from war/conflict, not
their own opportunities.**
"Cognitive warfare" and election influence make for a vivid transition
slide but don't connect directly to either proposed opportunity (feed
goals, clip labeling) -- the throughline from extreme to opportunity is
looser here than in the other three decks.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test the throughline.**
   *"Your absurd extreme is about political influence and 15-hour scroll
   days. How does 'let users set feed goals' actually address either of
   those?"*
   Tests whether the opportunity was derived from the extreme or bolted on
   afterward.

2. **Force a choice among the four unpursued cracks.**
   *"Of the four open problems you didn't turn into opportunities -- age
   verification, transparency tools, no market for a good algorithm, AI
   mechanics migrating to chatbots -- which is the strongest one you left
   on the table?"*
   Surfaces whether they triaged deliberately or just ran out of slide
   space.

## Team: AI-Generated Media and Entertainment

**Team:** Daniel Sung, Flora Huang, Steven Li, Owen Bartlett
**Domain:** Generative media, authenticity, and provenance
**Methodology:** Trending to the Absurd
**Submitted:** Week 2 deck (week24)

### Summary

Team traced generative video/image tools from small VFX assists (Netflix
cutting one sequence's production time 10x) toward fully synthetic content,
and carried the trend to a world where AI-generated media is
indistinguishable from human-made content -- collapsing the $649B US media
industry's gatekeeping role. Produced three explicitly *sequenced*
opportunities: a provenance/attestation standard, an authenticity-premium
certification market that depends on the first existing, and an
accountability layer for publishing at machine scale.

### Strengths

**Best-structured opportunity logic of the four teams.**
Each opportunity is run through real/important/addressable explicitly, and
critically, the team sequenced them ("certification needs the provenance
gap closing first") rather than presenting three independent bets. That's
systems thinking about their own portfolio, not just three separate ideas.

**Stakes extend past entertainment.**
Connected the domain to video evidence in court (80% of crime cases) and
customer-support dispute evidence -- shows the team looked for where the
extreme actually bites outside its home industry.

### Weaknesses

**Attribution gap.**
Only one name ("Daniel") is recoverable from the deck's speaker notes
against four listed team members. Confirm individual contribution before
grading four names equally.

**Opportunity 1 assumes a willing first mover with no candidate named.**
"What's missing is a standard and a first adopter -- commercial, not
research" is the exact right diagnosis, but the deck doesn't name a
plausible first adopter (a platform, an insurer, a camera manufacturer).
Worth pushing on who moves first and why they'd bear the cost alone.

### Suggested Questions (pick 2-3)

1. **Test the sequencing logic.**
   *"You said the authenticity-premium market can't exist until the
   provenance gap closes. Argue the other side -- could a market emerge
   without a standard, informally, first?"*
   Tests whether the dependency claim is reasoned or just asserted for
   narrative neatness.

2. **Name the first mover.**
   *"Who is the single most plausible first adopter of a provenance
   standard -- and what do they gain by moving before anyone else has to?"*
   Moves the strongest opportunity from "real and important" toward
   "addressable."

## Cross-Student Intelligence

**Domain overlap detected: three of four teams are converging on the same
underlying question from different angles.**

Public Approval of AI, Social Media Algorithms, and AI-Generated Media are
all, at root, asking what happens when an AI-adjacent system scales past
the institutions built to govern it -- legitimacy/trust (Team 2),
legal/regulatory liability (Team 3), and provenance/authenticity (Team 4).
These teams could cross-pollinate at the opportunity stage even though
their surface domains (public opinion, algorithms, media) look unrelated.
Smart Cities / Rapid Delivery is the one team working entirely outside this
cluster -- worth naming both facts to the class explicitly.

**Potential team-formation cluster:** Teams 2, 3, and 4 for any AI-
governance / accountability portfolio direction, if the course moves
toward team formation around shared threads rather than shared surface
domains.
