Clusters

A cluster is a place where several students independently converged on the same opportunity space, usually while exploring different domains. The class has detected 28 of these (18 through Week 2, 5 in Week 3, and 5 in Week 4); the eleven below group closely related clusters together and explain each one at more length, including where the same underlying problem shows up outside the class's own domains. Each card names which original clusters it combines.

Strong Signal = 4+ students across 2+ domains
Emerging = 3-4 students

Convergence across independent explorations is a strong validation signal.

AI Trust and Verification Infrastructure combines 4 clusters, spanning 9 of 15 domains

Strong Signal

This groups three related clusters -- provenance/chain-of-custody, machine legibility, and confidence-gating -- because they're all versions of the same problem: proving something is real, unaltered, or safe before someone downstream acts on it. Chain-of-custody already exists as a formal discipline outside anything the class touched: pharma has it for anti-counterfeiting, diamonds and luxury goods have it for authenticity certification, courts have it for evidence, blood and organ transport have it because a broken chain is fatal, and food safety has it as cold-chain tracking. AI adds two problems those older disciplines never had to solve: content provenance (proving a video or image wasn't generated) and machine legibility (making something only a person could judge into something a system can query).

A team could take an existing, well-understood chain-of-custody discipline -- pharma, diamonds, courtroom evidence -- and rebuild it for the AI era, or take the newly urgent problem (media provenance, machine-queryable legibility) and build the standard nobody's built yet. Confidence-gating -- blocking an irreversible action until confidence clears a threshold -- is the enforcement layer this whole area eventually needs, whichever piece you pick.

Week 3 added a fourth cluster, "Gate the Act, Don't Just Log It," which is the confidence-gating piece applied to oversight. The Agency Under Surveillance team found that every existing control on plate-reader searches is an audit log, and a log only reports what already happened: one deputy searched one woman's plate 1,639 times before anyone noticed. The Cybersecurity team found that attackers now break out of a network in 29 minutes, faster than any after-the-fact review of an AI agent's actions. Both teams concluded that the check has to sit in front of the action. The other surveillance team's privacy-first plate readers rely on logging every search, so the class now holds both positions.

The three original clusters -- Proof of Origin at Every Handoff, Making an Opaque System Remotely Legible, and Confidence-Gated Decision Layers -- surfaced independently across seven of the first eight domains the class explored: Smart Cities, AI Media, Social Media, Legal, Travel, Defense, and Accessibility. The Week 3 cluster added Surveillance and Cybersecurity, for nine of the fifteen domains explored so far.

AI Governance, Liability, and Insurance combines 5 clusters, spanning 8 of 15 domains

Strong Signal

This groups the class's "AI Outgrows the Institutions Built to Govern It," "Liability With No Holder," and "Verify-Before-Deploy" clusters, because all three are angles on the same open question: who's liable when an AI system is wrong, and how does anyone price that risk. Every industry that has adopted AI for a consequential decision is running into it. Healthcare has it with diagnostic algorithms. Auto insurers have it with autonomous vehicles. Hiring has it with resume-screening tools already facing discrimination lawsuits. Criminal justice has it with sentencing risk-assessment tools. Financial services has it with robo-advisors and automated underwriting. None of these have settled case law, and none have a mature insurance product.

A team could pick any one of those verticals -- healthcare AI liability, autonomous-vehicle insurance, hiring-algorithm audit compliance -- and build the accountability ledger or certification product for it specifically. Legal and Defense already proved two working versions of the underlying business model (verify the output, sell the verification to whoever underwrites the risk); the opportunity is applying that model somewhere nobody's tried yet.

Week 3 added two clusters. "Verify Before It Acts" joined the Who Watches the Watchers team, which wants independent accuracy and bias tests before a predictive policing model scores anyone (one model's predictions matched under 1% of crimes), with the Cybersecurity team, which found that nothing currently detects when an AI agent reaches its goal by a route nobody intended. "Risk No One Can Price Yet" came from the Supply Chain team's pick: no instrument lets importers, carriers, or marine insurers price or hedge the closure of a strait, even as Hormuz traffic fell from 100+ ships a day to about 5. That is the same pricing gap outside AI entirely, which suggests the underlying business (measure a new risk, sell the measurement to whoever underwrites it) is not limited to AI.
