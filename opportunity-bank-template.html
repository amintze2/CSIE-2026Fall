AI Trust and Verification Infrastructure combines 3 clusters, spanning 7 of 8 domains

Strong Signal

This groups three related clusters -- provenance/chain-of-custody, machine legibility, and confidence-gating -- because they're all versions of the same problem: proving something is real, unaltered, or safe before someone downstream acts on it. Chain-of-custody already exists as a formal discipline outside anything the class touched: pharma has it for anti-counterfeiting, diamonds and luxury goods have it for authenticity certification, courts have it for evidence, blood and organ transport have it because a broken chain is fatal, and food safety has it as cold-chain tracking. AI adds two problems those older disciplines never had to solve: content provenance (proving a video or image wasn't generated) and machine legibility (making something only a person could judge into something a system can query).

A team could take an existing, well-understood chain-of-custody discipline -- pharma, diamonds, courtroom evidence -- and rebuild it for the AI era, or take the newly urgent problem (media provenance, machine-queryable legibility) and build the standard nobody's built yet. Confidence-gating -- blocking an irreversible action until confidence clears a threshold -- is the enforcement layer this whole area eventually needs, whichever piece you pick.

The three original clusters -- Proof of Origin at Every Handoff, Making an Opaque System Remotely Legible, and Confidence-Gated Decision Layers -- surfaced independently across seven of the eight domains the class explored: Smart Cities, AI Media, Social Media, Legal, Travel, Defense, and Accessibility.

AI Governance, Liability, and Insurance combines 3 clusters, spanning 5 of 8 domains

Strong Signal

This groups the class's "AI Outgrows the Institutions Built to Govern It," "Liability With No Holder," and "Verify-Before-Deploy" clusters, because all three are angles on the same open question: who's liable when an AI system is wrong, and how does anyone price that risk. Every industry that has adopted AI for a consequential decision is running into it. Healthcare has it with diagnostic algorithms. Auto insurers have it with autonomous vehicles. Hiring has it with resume-screening tools already facing discrimination lawsuits. Criminal justice has it with sentencing risk-assessment tools. Financial services has it with robo-advisors and automated underwriting. None of these have settled case law, and none have a mature insurance product.

A team could pick any one of those verticals -- healthcare AI liability, autonomous-vehicle insurance, hiring-algorithm audit compliance -- and build the accountability ledger or certification product for it specifically. Legal and Defense already proved two working versions of the underlying business model (verify the output, sell the verification to whoever underwrites the risk); the opportunity is applying that model somewhere nobody's tried yet.

The three clusters surfaced across Public Approval, Social Media, AI Media, Legal, and Defense -- five of the eight domains the class explored.

Domains: Public Approval of AI Addictive and Opaque Social Media Algorithms AI-Generated Media and Entertainment Legal Defense and National Security

Personalization Built From the Edge Case Instead of the Average User combines 2 clusters, spanning 4 of 8 domains

Strong Signal

This groups "Declared Intent vs. Inferred Behavior" and "One-Size-Fits-All Personalization Breaks Down at the Edges." Most personalization gets built around an average user, then generalized outward -- and that ordering quietly breaks the product for anyone off the average. Healthcare treatment plans built around a clinical-trial-average patient underperform for anyone outside that population. Education software built for a typical learner fails students with different processing speeds. Financial products built around a standard credit history exclude the underbanked entirely. In every one of these fields, flipping the build order -- design for the hardest case first, generalize outward -- has repeatedly produced a better product for everyone.

A team could pick a personalization product built around the average case in a field that hasn't had this reckoning yet -- lending, education, healthcare, hiring -- and rebuild its interface starting from its hardest edge case. Or build the declared-preference layer as a standalone product: let people explicitly state a goal instead of a system inferring one from behavior it might be reading wrong.

Two class teams reached this from opposite failure modes -- Social Media found inference actively wrong, Accessibility found the generic interface actively excluding -- across four of the class's eight domains.

Distributed Node Networks Replacing Centralized Infrastructure combines 2 clusters, spanning 3 of 8 domains

Strong Signal

This groups "The Neighborhood as a Distributed Network" and "Cheap Distributed Sensing Beats Expensive Centralized Systems." The underlying pattern -- many cheap, distributed, software-coordinated nodes beating one expensive centralized asset -- is one of the oldest architectures in infrastructure, and it keeps reappearing as sensors and connectivity get cheap enough for a new field to try it. Disaster response is moving from centralized command centers to self-organizing mesh communication after a hurricane or earthquake knocks out central infrastructure. Clean energy is moving from centralized power plants toward distributed microgrids. Agricultural monitoring for smallholder farmers is shifting from expensive centralized satellite contracts to cheap, distributed ground sensors.

A team could take the architecture directly into any of those fields, or build the general coordination software -- the fusion layer that makes a mesh of cheap, independent nodes act like one reliable system -- that any of them could plug into.

Three class teams independently rebuilt pieces of this architecture -- a counter-drone sensor mesh, a peer-to-peer delivery network, and Week 1's Travel readability layer -- across three of the eight domains explored.

Simulated Training and Practice Infrastructure for Disappearing Apprenticeships 1 cluster, spanning 2 of 8 domains

Strong Signal

Formerly "Rebuilding the Low-Stakes Practice Layer." Every field with an apprenticeship model is losing its low-stakes practice layer to automation at roughly the same rate, not just software engineering. Medical residents already train on cadavers and simulators because real patients can't absorb the risk of a first attempt -- that same logic is now overdue in fields that never had it. Pilots train on flight simulators; drone and autonomous-vehicle operators mostly don't have an equivalent yet. Skilled trades still rely on shadowing a senior tradesperson, a model that breaks as fewer seniors exist to shadow.

A team could pick one of these under-simulated fields and build its practice environment specifically, or build a general-purpose simulation platform -- the digital-twin approach Defense already validated -- that multiple fields could plug their own scenarios into.

It showed up in the class's own work as both a talent-pipeline problem (Public Approval) and a systems-testing problem (Defense), independently, a week apart, across two of the eight domains explored.

Provenance and Authenticity Verification Across Delivered Packages, AI Video, and Sponsored Content Labels combines 2 connections

Smart Cities / Rapid Delivery trust and verification layer <--> AI-Generated Media and Entertainment provenance gap <--> Addictive and Opaque Social Media Algorithms motive labels

Combines "One Chain-of-Custody Standard for Parcels and Pixels" and "Motive Labels That Can Be Audited." Proving an instantly delivered package hasn't been tampered with, proving a video wasn't generated, and proving a "paid political ad" label is honest are the same underlying claim: something is what it says it is, made when and by whom it says. All three currently fail the same way -- the claim is self-asserted, and nothing separates an honest one from a cheap one. The fix is identical everywhere: a signed record written at the moment of creation or handoff, checked at each point the object or content changes hands. This kind of infrastructure already exists outside anything the class touched -- pharmaceutical anti-counterfeiting, diamond certification, courtroom chain-of-custody -- proof the pattern works, just not yet applied to delivery, media, or advertising.

A team could build a single audited-claims layer that certifies more than one type of claim at once ("this is human-made," "this hasn't been tampered with," "this is who paid for it"), since the underlying verification -- signed records, checked at handoff -- is identical regardless of which claim it's attached to. Delivery fleets, which already log every transfer and already face regulatory pressure to prove custody, are a more plausible first customer than media companies, which have less operational infrastructure to build on.

Built from: Smart Cities' trust layer (Yash Chadda, Sichen Deng, Kendall Frederick, Jonathan Locala); AI Media's provenance gap and authenticity premium (Daniel Sung, Flora Huang, Steven Li, Owen Bartlett); Social Media's motive labels (Jingchen Jiang, Sahana Raja, Sophia Mai, Sylvia Yam)

Public Approval of AI AI tracker (accountability) <--> AI-Generated Media and Entertainment accountability vacuum

Formerly "The Tracker Feeds the Underwriter." Insurance can't price a risk it has no loss data for, and loss data for AI-caused harm barely exists because nobody consistently records who built, deployed, and approved the system that caused it. Applying the Curb/Rooftop Exchange Model to Grid Interconnection Capacity 1 connection

Public Approval of AI who pays for the power <--> Smart Cities / Rapid Delivery logistics real-estate exchange

Formerly "Large-Load Capacity Exchange." Smart Cities proposed a market for reserving and pricing scarce curb, rooftop, and skylane access. Adding a Confidence Gate in Front of Machine-Scale AI Publishing 1 connection

AI-Generated Media and Entertainment accountability vacuum <--> Accessibility and Assistive Technology Week 1 confidence-gated AAC channel

Formerly "Gating Machine-Scale Publishing." The AAC channel won't commit an utterance until a confidence layer clears it, built to protect against acting on an ambiguous signal too early. Machine-scale AI publishing has no equivalent gate, which is precisely why no insurer will absorb its tail risk -- there's no controllable process to price, only an unbounded one. Putting a confidence gate in front of publication (low-confidence output routes to human review, the rest ships) gives an underwriter something measurable instead of something they have to trust blind.

This same gate -- block the irreversible action until confidence clears a threshold -- is reusable well past publishing: automated content moderation, algorithmic loan approval, and AI-drafted medical documentation could all use the identical mechanism. A team could build the gate as general infrastructure other AI products plug in front of their own commit actions, rather than a bespoke solution for publishing alone.

Built from: Daniel Sung, Flora Huang, Steven Li, Owen Bartlett (AI Media); Jingchen Jiang, Kendall Frederick, Sissi Lai, Sophia Mai (Accessibility, Week 1)

Wiring Accessibility's Real-Time Communication Tools Into Legal Triage and Courtroom Hearings combines 2 connections

Accessibility and Assistive Technology confidence-gated AAC, real-time captioning <--> Legal pretrial triage, AI defense for debt/eviction cases

Combines "Accessible Crisis & Court Navigator" and "Live-Captioned Debt & Eviction Hearings." Legal's pretrial triage service reminds people of court dates and routes them to what they need instead of a bail bond -- but a deaf, hard-of-hearing, or communication-disabled defendant is disproportionately likely to miss exactly this kind of time-critical, high-stakes interaction in the first place. And Legal's mass AI defense service represents defendants at a scale no attorney could match one-on-one, but the hearing itself is still spoken, real-time, and unforgiving of a missed word. Wiring Accessibility's confidence-gated, real-time-speed communication and low-latency captioning directly into both -- the court-facing triage and the live hearing feed -- turns the accessibility layer from a generic add-on into the thing that makes the legal service actually reach the population most likely to fall through the gap it was built to close.

The same pairing generalizes to any legal, medical, or government process that's real-time, high-stakes, and currently assumes everyone can follow spoken language at full speed -- immigration hearings, medical consent conversations, DMV or benefits-office interactions.

Built from: Jingchen Jiang, Kendall Frederick, Sissi Lai, Sophia Mai (Accessibility and Assistive Technology); Owen Bartlett (Legal)

Adding Accessible Inputs and Confidence-Gated Commits to Group Travel Planning combines 2 connections

Accessibility and Assistive Technology spatial audio, gesture input, confidence-gated AAC <--> Travel social/collaborative group itineraries

Combines "Mixed-Ability Collaborative Trip Planning" and "Group-Confidence-Gated Itinerary Commits." Travel's collaborative-itinerary tool assumes every group member interacts with the plan the same way, and has no protection at the moment that actually costs money -- a non-refundable booking locking in the instant one person clicks. Accessibility already solved both halves of this, for a different product. Its spatial-audio translation and tremor-tolerant gesture input could be fed in as first-class collaborative inputs, so a blind or motor-impaired traveler gets an equal vote instead of a bolted-on accessibility mode. And its confidence-gated AAC channel -- which won't commit an utterance until confidence clears a threshold -- is the exact mechanism missing from group bookings: aggregate the whole group's confidence before an irreversible commit fires, not just the fastest clicker's.

Nobody in the class has explicitly claimed either half of this pairing yet, which makes it a genuinely open build, not just a strong signal -- a team could take the input-accessibility half or the confidence-gating half and still have a complete, presentable product.

Current contributors: Sylvia, Steven, Flora, Yash (Travel); Jingchen Jiang, Kendall Frederick, Sissi Lai, Sophia Mai (Accessibility and Assistive Technology). Unclaimed -- watch for someone to build it.

Applying Accessibility Input Research to Combat Operator Consoles combines 2 connections

Accessibility and Assistive Technology tremor-tolerant input, spatial-audio translation <--> Defense and National Security digital-twin cyber range, sensor-fusion mesh

Combines "Degraded-Input Operator Consoles" and "Battlefield Spatial-Audio Threat Awareness." A soldier operating a drone or targeting console under stress, injury, cold, or in bulky gloves has functionally the same input problem as Accessibility's tremor- and reach-tolerant kiosk work: partial, noisy motion that still needs to become a confident, real-time command. And an operator working in smoke, at night, or under emissions control needs the same eyes-free situational read that Accessibility's spatial-audio work gives a blind traveler -- direction and intensity as sound, not a screen. Both are the identical accessibility research aimed at a different user under different constraints.

A team could field either piece of accessibility research as combat-console UX directly, validated in the same digital-twin cyber range Defense already built before it's trusted in a real system -- accessibility research turns out to be a legitimate pipeline into defense-tech interface design, not a separate category.

Built from: Group 3 -- Sahana, Kerry, Michael, Penn, Evan (Defense and National Security); Jingchen Jiang, Kendall Frederick, Sissi Lai, Sophia Mai (Accessibility and Assistive Technology). Unclaimed -- watch for someone to build it.

Applying Legal's Verified-Filing Model to Travel Compensation Claims and Overlooked-Party Representation combines 2 connections

Legal verified-filing certification, AI defense for overlooked defendants <--> Travel cross-carrier rebooking, readability layer

Combines "Travel-Disruption Rights Autofiler" and "The Overlooked-Party Legibility Engine." Two separate connections between Legal and Travel point at the same underlying opportunity: giving a party too small or too rushed for individual attention the scale of representation an expert would normally provide. The moment Travel's rebooking layer detects a disruption -- a cancellation, a denied boarding, a carrier-caused delay -- that event is very often also a compensation-eligible legal claim almost no traveler actually files, because doing so requires legal knowledge they don't have and time they don't want to spend. Attach a verified-filing service, built the same way as Legal's malpractice-insurer certification, so the rebooking product doesn't just get someone rebooked -- it auto-files what they're owed.

The second connection is more structural: strip away the domain, and Legal's debt/eviction defense and Travel's small-destination readability layer are the same engine -- take a party too small, poor, or obscure for individual institutional attention, and give it a standardized way to become legible and actionable to an AI system at scale. That's a reusable pattern with an open third instantiation: a gig worker invisible to a benefits system, a rural clinic invisible to a hospital network -- built twice this class already, generic enough to build a third time.

Built from: Owen Bartlett (Legal); Sylvia, Steven, Flora, Yash (Travel)
